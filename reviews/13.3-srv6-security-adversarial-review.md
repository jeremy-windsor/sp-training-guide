# Adversarial Review: `13.3-srv6-security.md`

## Verdict

This module is **strong in intent** and gets the **core SRv6 security model broadly right**: boundary filtering of domain SIDs, per-node protection, SRH visibility gaps, BSID risk, and the fact that SRv6 has a materially larger default attack surface than SR-MPLS.

But for **IE-level / CCIE-SP-grade training**, it is **not safe to publish as-is**.

Why:
- Several claims are **overstated or imprecise** relative to the RFCs.
- Multiple config examples are **unsafe, incomplete, or likely syntactically wrong**.
- Some vendor/platform claims are **too specific to be unverified**.
- It omits a few **critical SRv6 security topics**, especially **single-segment/no-SRH traffic**, **control-plane attack surface**, and **inter-domain / multi-AS exposure**.

If this showed up in an IE workbook or was reviewed by a strong CCIE proctor, they would absolutely stab at a few spots until it bled.

---

## Executive Summary of Findings

### Publish blockers

1. **AH section overclaims what RFC 8754 says**.
2. **IOS-XR CoPP example is not a reliable XR CoPP config** and appears to mix CoPP/MPP concepts.
3. **ACL examples authorize the wrong source space** for RFC 8754 per-node protection.
4. **`permit icmp any any` on edge filters is too broad** for security guidance.
5. **Vendor support claims are too precise without hard validation**.
6. **The module misses a major attack class: SRv6 with no SRH** (single-segment / pure DA SID traffic).
7. **Firewall guidance oversimplifies policy evaluation** by implying the full segment list alone determines final delivery behavior.
8. **Some IOS-XR/Junos command examples are likely wrong or release-dependent enough to be unsafe in training material**.

### Overall score

- **Conceptual security model:** 8/10
- **RFC precision:** 6/10
- **Config safety:** 4/10
- **Vendor accuracy confidence:** 5/10
- **IE exam defensibility:** 6/10

# 1. Technical Accuracy Attacks

## 1.1 RFC 8754 §5.1 is described mostly correctly, but the module invents terminology

### What the module says
It refers to RFC 8754 §5.1 controls as **"Level 1"** and **"Level 2"** and calls destination filtering the **primary** control and per-node source validation the **secondary** control.

### Problem
RFC 8754 §5.1 does define **two numbered controls**:
1. drop packets entering the SR domain destined to SIDs in the domain
2. complement that with per-node protection based on source address

But the RFC does **not** name them "Level 1" and "Level 2" as formal terms, and it does not present them as a standards-defined hierarchy.

### Why a proctor would care
Because IE-level content should distinguish between:
- **RFC language**, and
- **author interpretation / operational prioritization**.

### Fix
Reword as:
- "RFC 8754 §5.1 defines two complementary protections" 
- then separately state, "Operationally, boundary destination filtering is usually your first and broadest control."

---

## 1.2 AH section overstates the RFC

### What the module says
It says RFC 8754 §7.5 is unambiguous and that **AH is not compatible with SRv6 SRH processing**.

### Problem
RFC 8754 §7.5 does **not** say "AH is incompatible" in that absolute way.
It says:
- the SR domain does not rely on AH to secure SRH
- **the use of SRH with AH is not defined in this document**
- future documents may define it

So the module turns **"not defined here"** into **"explicitly impossible"**.

### Nuance
The module's intuition is directionally reasonable: AH and SRH are awkward together because SR processing changes fields hop by hop and AH needs defined mutability rules. But that is **analysis**, not what RFC 8754 literally says.

### Fix
Replace with:
- "RFC 8754 does not define AH use with SRH. In practice, operators should not assume AH protects SRH semantics. Use SRH HMAC TLV for SRH authorization/integrity and ESP where payload protection is needed."

---

## 1.3 Hdr Ext Len explanation is imprecise

### What the module says
`Hdr Ext Len — length in 8-octet units - 1`

### Problem
That shorthand is too sloppy for expert training.
Per RFC 8200, extension header length is measured in **8-octet units, not including the first 8 octets**.

### Why it matters
People do packet math from this field. If you teach the wrong mental model, someone will eventually botch TLV sizing or forensic decoding.

### Fix
Use:
- "Hdr Ext Len = length of the SRH in 8-octet units, excluding the first 8 octets."

---

## 1.4 "Segments Left cannot be manipulated in transit without breaking forwarding" is too absolute

### Problem
A malicious device absolutely can alter `Segments Left` or the DA and still create a useful misroute, drop, or path perturbation. The field is mutable by design.

### Better statement
"Transit nodes are not supposed to inspect or modify SRH, and unauthorized modification will usually corrupt intended forwarding or fail HMAC validation if HMAC is enforced."

---

## 1.5 Firewall bypass section oversimplifies what a firewall would need to know

### What the module says
A firewall must "chase the full segment list to determine actual delivery destination".

### Problem
That is not sufficient in many real SRv6 cases.
Final outcome depends on:
- local behavior bound to the SID
- decapsulation behaviors (`End.DT4`, `End.DT6`, `End.DX*`)
- policy-bound behaviors (`End.B6.Encaps`)
- local control-plane knowledge of what a SID actually means

A firewall cannot reliably infer the security effect from the segment list alone unless it also understands the SID-to-behavior mapping.

### Fix
Say the firewall must either:
- be **SRv6-aware enough to interpret RH Type 4 plus local SID semantics**, or
- fail closed and block SRH from untrusted segments.

---

## 1.6 Scenario 2 overstates what route exposure reveals

### What the module says
It implies SID structure exposure via IGP/BGP lets attackers infer VRF tables and function layouts.

### Problem
Sometimes true, not inherently true.
What is typically advertised is:
- locators
- behavior metadata in control plane
- sometimes policy information

But **not every deployed SID/function is necessarily exposed in the simple form the text implies**, and table IDs are not inherently discoverable unless the operator encoded them into SID values or exposed them via control-plane artifacts.

### Fix
Qualify the claim:
- "If your SID plan embeds meaning in the SID value, topology and service role leakage becomes much easier."

---

## 1.7 HMAC section is directionally right, but too broad in wording

### What the module says
"Any modification to the SRH in transit invalidates the HMAC."

### Problem
That is broadly okay for training, but not precise enough for IE-level discussion.
RFC 8754 defines exactly what is covered and how verification is performed at the verifying node. The module also does not mention that HMAC verification is driven by **local configuration**, not universally mandatory behavior.

### Fix
Add:
- HMAC verification is **optional / policy driven**
- operators choose where it is required
- HMAC mainly validates the authorized segment list selection and integrity under the RFC-defined computation model

---

## 1.8 The module misses a critical SRv6 fact: SRv6 can exist without an SRH

### Problem
RFC 8754 explicitly allows a single-segment SR Policy with **no SRH at all** if no TLV/flag data is needed. In that case, the SID sits only in the IPv6 Destination Address.

### Why this matters
This is a huge security omission.
If a reader walks away thinking "block RH Type 4" is the SRv6 security control, they are already standing in the bear trap.

### Attack surface missed
- single-segment steering to a local SID
- direct delivery to `End`, `End.X`, `End.DT*`, etc. without SRH
- attacks not visible to RH4-based filters

### Fix
Add a dedicated section:
**"Single-segment SRv6 (no SRH) is still SRv6."**
Then explain that **destination-based filtering of the SID block** is the indispensable control because it catches both:
- SRH-bearing packets, and
- no-SRH packets addressed directly to SIDs.

# 2. Config Safety Review

## 2.1 The source-authorization logic is wrong for RFC 8754 per-node protection

### What the module config does
It defines:
- `SRV6_DOMAIN_SOURCES permit fc00::/32 le 128`
- then permits SRv6 from that source space

### Problem
RFC 8754 §5.1 item 2 does **not** say "authorize packets sourced from the SID block".
It says per-node protection should drop packets to local SIDs when the **source address is not from the internal interface-address space A/a**.

These are different things.

### Why this is dangerous
Many real SRv6 deployments use:
- one prefix plan for locators / SIDs
- another for loopbacks / infra / link addressing

If an operator copy-pastes this logic and only authorizes the SID block as source space, they can:
- wrongly drop legitimate internal traffic, or
- falsely assume protection exists when their actual infra sources are elsewhere.

### Fix
Use separate examples:
- **SID destination block** = locator/SID space
- **authorized source block(s)** = internal loopback / infra / host-origin prefixes allowed to source SR traffic

---

## 2.2 `permit icmp any any` is too broad for a security guide

### Why it's bad
On edge-facing iACLs, blanket ICMPv6 permit rules are sloppy and risky.
This admits far more than:
- Packet Too Big
- Time Exceeded
- Parameter Problem
- limited ND where appropriate

It can expose the box to unnecessary ICMPv6 surface.

### Fix
Be explicit by ICMPv6 type where platform supports it.
At minimum say:
- "Do not blindly permit all ICMPv6 on untrusted interfaces. Permit only the ICMPv6 types you operationally require."

---

## 2.3 `UPSTREAM_INGRESS_FILTER` contains a spoofing footgun

### What the example says
```text
10 permit ipv6 fc00:0::/32 any
20 deny ipv6 any fc00:0::/32
30 permit ipv6 any any
```

### Problem
On an upstream-facing **ingress** ACL, line 10 is a gift-wrapped spoofing hole.
An attacker can send traffic **into** your edge with a forged source inside `fc00:0::/32` and match the permit.

### Why the comment is wrong
The comment says this permits "your traffic going out and coming back".
That is not how an ingress ACL on the upstream interface should be modeled. Return traffic is not legitimized merely by spoofing your source space.

### Fix
Delete that permit from the example unless it is paired with a much stronger, clearly explained stateful or anti-spoofing model.

---

## 2.4 The IOS-XR CoPP example is not trustworthy

### Problems
The example appears to mix:
- classification syntax,
- CoPP/LPTS concepts,
- and management-plane protection style config.

Specific concerns:
- the class-map / match syntax is questionable for XR as shown
- the policy is never clearly attached in a real XR CoPP manner
- `control-plane -> management-plane inband -> interface ... allow ipv6 access-list` is not the same as an SRv6-specific CoPP policer

### Operational risk
An operator may think they have CoPP for SRv6, paste this, and actually have **nothing useful**.

### Fix
Either:
- replace with a verified XR example for the exact release family, or
- remove the config and keep the section conceptual.

---

## 2.5 Junos RE-protection example is incomplete and potentially unsafe if treated as a real lo0 filter

### Problems
- `LOCAL_SRV6_SIDS` is referenced but not defined.
- default term is `accept`, which is dangerous if a reader interprets this as a complete RE protection policy.
- filter context is unclear: transit interface vs `lo0` RE protection are very different use cases.

### Fix
State clearly whether the filter is:
- transit iACL, or
- RE-protect / loopback filter

If it is RE protection, the example must be part of a **default-deny** framework, not a blanket accept tail term.

---

## 2.6 Fragment handling advice is too blunt

### What the module says
It recommends denying all IPv6 fragmented traffic at untrusted ingress and implies legitimate SRv6 traffic should not be fragmented.

### Problem
That is a common hardening choice, but it is **not free**.
Blanket fragment drops can break legitimate IPv6 flows when PMTUD is impaired or when operational conditions are ugly, which they always are because the network is a haunted house.

### Fix
Present this as a **tradeoff**, not a universal best practice:
- preferred if your edge policy allows it and applications tolerate it
- validate PMTUD and operational impact before blanket enforcement

# 3. What a CCIE Examiner Would Flag

## 3.1 AH explanation is too absolute and not faithful to RFC wording
A proctor would likely say: "Show me where RFC 8754 says incompatible, not just undefined." 

## 3.2 RFC 8754 source-validation logic is misapplied in the example
Using the SID block as the source-authorization block is not the RFC model.

## 3.3 The module misses no-SRH SRv6
That is a big one. An examiner can easily ask:
- "How do you protect direct-to-SID traffic with no RH Type 4 present?"
If the lesson never taught that, it is incomplete.

## 3.4 CoPP section is not platform-trustworthy
IE content cannot hand-wave platform syntax when presenting literal config.

## 3.5 Vendor verification commands look shaky
Likely flags:
- XR `show access-lists ipv6 ... hardware ingress interface ...`
- Junos `show route table inet6 protocol sr-srv6`
- Junos operational syntax around `show srv6 ...`
- XR `traceroute srv6 policy name <policy-name>`
- Junos `ping routing-instance ... routing-options source ...`

Any of those may be valid on some build somewhere in the cave system, but this module presents them too confidently.

## 3.6 SR-MPLS comparison table is too absolutist
Examples a proctor might challenge:
- "Every firewall understands MPLS" -> false / exaggerated
- "Non-MPLS nodes drop MPLS traffic" -> too broad
- "HMAC protection N/A" -> simplistic comparison

## 3.7 Hardware claims use suspect platform terminology
Particularly:
- "ASR 9000 with 64-bit line cards"
- Typhoon/Trident wording
- precise Junos MPC support matrix without source validation

That is exactly the kind of thing a strong reviewer circles in red.

# 4. Missing Critical Content

## 4.1 Single-segment / no-SRH SRv6 traffic
**Most important omission in the whole module.**

Needed content:
- RFC 8754 allows omitting SRH when only one segment is needed
- SRv6 security is therefore not reducible to RH4 filtering
- destination-based SID-block filtering is the truly universal edge control

---

## 4.2 Control-plane attack surface
The module focuses almost entirely on data-plane injection.
It undercovers attacks on the control plane that make the data plane dangerous in the first place:
- forged or leaked SRv6 locator advertisement
- incorrect SRv6 behavior codepoint advertisement
- BGP SR Policy distribution abuse
- IGP poisoning / route leaks exposing locators beyond intended domain

For IE-level security, this omission is real.

---

## 4.3 Inter-domain / multi-AS SRv6 security
If SRv6 crosses trust boundaries, the security model changes materially.
Missing topics:
- inter-AS locator exposure
- policy exchange to peers
- BSID exposure beyond administrative domain
- what "trusted domain" means when the domain stops being neat and hypothetical

---

## 4.4 SID plan security under RFC 9602
The module cites RFC 9602 but does not really incorporate its significance.
It should discuss:
- dedicated SRv6 SID considerations in the IPv6 addressing architecture
- why operator addressing choices affect filterability, observability, and leak risk

---

## 4.5 SRv6 behavior risk hierarchy
Not all SIDs are equally dangerous.
The lesson should explicitly rank exposure risk, roughly like:
1. **Highest risk:** `End.DT*`, `End.DX*`, `End.B6*`, anything inserting into VRFs/policies/services
2. **Medium:** `End.X`, `End.T`
3. **Lower:** plain `End`

Right now the module hints at this, but does not formalize it.

---

## 4.6 Extension-header chain evasion beyond fragmentation
Missing:
- long EH chains
- multiple Dest-Opts / HBH / RH ordering issues
- parser-depth / TCAM limitations
- cases where hardware sees IPv6 but not deep enough to reach RH4

Fragmentation is only one evasion path.

---

## 4.7 OAM and telemetry exposure
Missing or underdeveloped:
- SRv6 OAM misuse for reconnaissance
- trace / path diagnostics as recon tools
- NetFlow/IPFIX observability gaps for SRH and uSID
- logging blind spots when PSP/USD strip evidence before inspection points

---

## 4.8 Internal hostile host model deserves more depth
The module mentions compromised internal hosts, but does not sufficiently explain the delegated-validation model from RFC 8754 §5.2.
That is important in real DC / host-SRv6 environments.

---

# 5. Vendor Claim Verification Challenges

## 5.1 Cisco IOS-XR section contains multiple claims that need hard source validation
Potentially weak / risky claims:
- exact ACL keyword availability (`routing-type`, `extension-header`, `ipv6-extension-header`) across releases
- NCS 5500 / ASR 9000 support matrix as written
- "ASR 9000 with 64-bit line cards" terminology
- uSID support list as presented
- XR verification command examples

### Recommendation
Do not publish exact support matrices unless each bullet is traceable to:
- release notes,
- platform config guides,
- or lab validation.

---

## 5.2 Junos section likely mixes real capability with unverified syntax assumptions
Potentially shaky:
- `routing-header-type 4` support details by MPC generation
- `show srv6 ...` / `show spring srv6 ...` operational syntax claims
- `show route table inet6 protocol sr-srv6`
- the statement tying `seg-routing-mpls` and `source-packet-routing` together in an SRv6 context

### Recommendation
Reduce to capability statements unless exact syntax is lab-verified.

---

## 5.3 Nokia SR OS claim is too casual for IE-grade material
The line:
- `ipv6 acl ... routing-ext-hdr rh-type 4`

may be right, wrong, or right only on certain releases. Right now it reads like a confident fact without proof.

### Recommendation
Either verify with SR OS docs or cut it.

---

## 5.4 PAN/FortiGate section is too speculative
Phrases like:
- "can parse SRH headers in application identification"
- "SRH filtering can be achieved via custom application filters"
- "official support documentation is sparse"

are not strong enough for expert training.

### Why this is dangerous
It can create false confidence that the firewall is SRv6-aware enough when it may not be.

### Fix
Make the section more conservative:
- "Assume fail-open unless the vendor explicitly documents RH4 parsing and policy semantics for your platform and software train, and you have tested it."

---

# 6. Contradictions and Internal Tension

## 6.1 The RFC model says per-node protection is on internal interfaces, but the example emphasis is mostly edge-facing
The text explains RFC 8754 item 2 correctly, but the config examples and narrative weight are edge-heavy.
A student could leave believing external ingress ACLs are the main real config and internal per-node iACLs are optional garnish.

## 6.2 The text says CoPP is distinct from boundary ACLs, but the example is not a clean CoPP example
So the conceptual point is right, but the example undermines it.

## 6.3 The lesson warns against overtrusting RH4 visibility, but does not explicitly tie that to no-SRH single-segment traffic
That leaves a hole in the student's mental model.

---

# 7. Dangerous Advice

## 7.1 Broad ICMP permits on untrusted ingress
Already covered above. This is the easiest copy-paste footgun in the lesson.

## 7.2 Upstream ingress permit for traffic sourced from the SID block
This is the nastiest config error in the module.
It can directly create a spoofing bypass.

## 7.3 Overconfidence in speculative firewall support
If a reader interprets the PAN/FortiGate section as "the firewall probably handles it," security gets worse.

## 7.4 Copy-pasting unverified vendor syntax into production edge filters
Bad ACL syntax at an edge is how you meet change control in the afterlife.

## 7.5 Overstating fragment drops as universally safe
Can absolutely break legitimate IPv6 traffic if rolled out blindly.

# 8. Completeness Check on the 7 Attack Scenarios

The module's 7 scenarios are useful, but not complete.

## Good scenarios already covered
- SRH injection through domain SIDs
- topology enumeration
- firewall bypass
- transparent transit exposure
- BSID abuse
- fragmentation evasion
- processing amplification

## Important missing scenarios

### 8.1 Single-segment/no-SRH SID injection
A packet can target a local SID with **no SRH present**.
This is a first-class attack scenario and belongs in the module.

### 8.2 Control-plane poisoning / locator leak
If the attacker can expose or redirect locator reachability, many data-plane controls are bypassed or weakened.

### 8.3 Malicious or compromised internal SR headend
A trusted internal headend can generate perfectly valid SRH or no-SRH traffic. That is a different threat from random outside injection.

### 8.4 Deep extension-header parser exhaustion
Not just fragmentation. Long EH chains can defeat shallow inspection hardware.

### 8.5 Inter-domain SRv6 / peer-domain abuse
Especially relevant if SRv6 is used beyond a tightly bounded internal core.

### 8.6 SRv6 OAM / recon abuse
Operators use OAM; attackers read the same manuals.

### 8.7 Behavior-specific abuse hierarchy
A dedicated scenario on `End.DT*` / `End.B6*` / service insertion SIDs would help because those are materially worse than plain `End`.

# 10. Bottom Line

This module has the right **instincts** and a solid **security posture bias**, which is rarer than unicorns and competent change windows.

But for **IE-level training**, instinct is not enough.
It needs another pass for:
- RFC fidelity,
- config correctness,
- platform precision,
- and one glaring completeness gap: **SRv6 without SRH**.

If you fix those, this becomes strong.
If you do not, it teaches the right paranoia with just enough wrong detail to get someone embarrassed in a lab or hurt in production.
