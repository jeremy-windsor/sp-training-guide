## Overview

> Every outage has a moment where someone says "I think it's fine now" and then it isn't. Every postmortem has a section titled "Timeline" that is quietly fictional because nobody thought to capture timestamps until 20 minutes into the incident. Every RCA has a root cause that is actually the third thing that went wrong, because the first two were invisible. This section exists because incident response in a service provider backbone is a skill — not a personality trait, not "being good under pressure," but a repeatable discipline with structures, checklists, and forensic methods that work whether you're calm, panicking, or on hour four of a bridge call with a customer who is also panicking.

**What you'll learn:**
- The first five minutes: triage framework that separates control plane from data plane, growing from contained, and backbone from edge
- Structured incident classification for SP networks — not ITIL theater, but severity models that drive the right response at the right speed
- Protocol-specific diagnostic flows for BGP incidents, IGP storms, MPLS/RSVP-TE failures, SRv6 path issues, and optical layer faults
- Packet capture and label/SID tracing in production — where to tap, how to decode, and what 100G+ speeds do to your capture strategy
- Failure cascade mechanics: how a single fiber cut becomes a five-site outage through preemption, reconvergence, and overload
- Post-incident forensics: timeline reconstruction from logs, telemetry, and packet captures — building an honest picture from dishonest data
- RCA methodology that finds actual root causes instead of blaming the last person who typed something
- Communication during outages: what the NOC needs, what engineering needs, what customers need, and why they are three different messages

**Scope note:** This is not a disaster recovery or business continuity document. This is how an operations team handles a backbone incident from the first alarm to the signed postmortem. If you want to know how to drain a node before maintenance, that's Section 13.1. If you want to understand how RSVP-TE fails, that's Section 13.4. This section is for when something has already failed and you need to figure out what, why, and how to make it stop.

## 1. The First Five Minutes

The first five minutes of a backbone incident determine whether the outage lasts 15 minutes or 4 hours. Not because of technical complexity — because of human behavior. The wrong triage path sends three engineers debugging DNS while a fiber cut bleeds traffic across two regions.

### The Triage Decision Tree

Every backbone incident reduces to four questions asked in order:

```
                         SOMETHING IS WRONG
                              │
                   ┌──────────┴──────────┐
                   │                     │
             Is it growing?         Is it contained?
                   │                     │
            ┌──────┴──────┐              │
            │             │              │
       Actively         Stable        Contained
       spreading       but wide       single site
            │             │              │
      SEVERITY 1     SEVERITY 2     SEVERITY 3
      All hands       War room       On-call
      Escalate NOW    Escalate 5m    Diagnose
```

But before you can classify severity, you need to answer the real first question:

**Is this control plane, data plane, or both?**

```
SYMPTOM                                    → LIKELY PLANE

Routes disappeared from RIB/FIB            → Control plane
Interfaces up, forwarding black-holed      → Data plane
BGP sessions flapping                      → Control plane
Packet loss on specific prefix, path exists → Data plane
IGP adjacency cycling                      → Control plane (or physical)
MPLS labels present, traffic dropped       → Data plane (label mismatch, MTU)
SRv6 SID reachable, traffic misrouted      → Data plane (SRH processing)
Everything is wrong simultaneously         → Physical layer or control plane storm
```

This distinction matters because control-plane incidents and data-plane incidents have different diagnostic flows, different escalation paths, and very different first-aid measures. Trying to fix a data-plane black hole by restarting BGP is how you turn a localized problem into a widespread one.

### Question Two: How Big Is the Blast Radius?

Once you know the plane, scope the damage:

```
SCOPE CHECK                              HOW TO CHECK

Single link/interface?                    show interface brief | grep down
                                          show isis adjacency | include Down
                                          show ospf neighbor | include Down

Single node?                              Is the management plane reachable?
                                          Are ALL adjacencies on this node affected?
                                          Check NMS/telemetry for node-level alerts

Single region/POP?                        Are multiple nodes in the same site affected?
                                          Is the transport ring that feeds this POP intact?
                                          Check for common-cause: power, fiber, HVAC

Multi-region / backbone-wide?             Multiple IGP areas reporting adjacency loss?
                                          BGP route withdrawals across diverse prefixes?
                                          This is a Severity 1. Stop diagnosing. Start coordinating.
```

### Question Three: Is It Getting Worse?

This is the question people skip. They see a problem, they start fixing it, and they don't notice that the blast radius tripled while they were in a CLI session.

**Monitoring the spread:**

For real-time triage, streaming telemetry (gNMI) dashboards are superior to CLI polling when available. The commands below are for when you need direct CLI verification:

```
# IOS-XR: Watch for new adjacency losses in real time
# Re-run periodically (every 5-10 seconds) or use streaming telemetry
show isis adjacency
# Junos equivalent below also works on a manual refresh interval

# Junos: Monitor routing table for active withdrawals  
monitor start /var/log/rpd
    (watch for: RPD_ISIS_ADJACENCY, bgp_read_message: UPDATE withdraw)

# Either platform: Count active routes — is the number declining?
# IOS-XR
show route summary | include Total
    (repeat every 30 seconds)

# Junos
show route summary | match "Total"
    (repeat every 30 seconds)
```

If the route count is declining or adjacency losses are accumulating, the incident is growing. Stop debugging the original failure and switch to containment.

### Question Four: What Changed Recently?

The most common root cause of backbone outages is "someone did something." Before you assume it's hardware, cosmic rays, or the phase of the moon:

```
# IOS-XR: Last configuration commits
show configuration commit list | utility head 20

# Junos: Last configuration changes
show system commit

# Either: Recent RSVP-TE events (LSP changes often follow maintenance)
# IOS-XR
show mpls traffic-eng tunnels brief | include Down|Reopt

# Junos
show mpls lsp brief | match Dn

# Check: Was there a maintenance window in the last 4 hours?
# Check: Did someone push a config change through automation?
# Check: Did a software upgrade happen on any node in the path?
```

If a change happened, the fastest path is to revert it and confirm the symptoms clear before doing any deeper analysis. You can do root cause analysis after the bleeding stops.

### The Five-Minute Checklist

Print this. Tape it to the monitor. Use it every time.

```
□ MINUTE 0-1: CONFIRM AND CLASSIFY
  - Confirm the alarm is real (not a monitoring false positive)
  - Control plane, data plane, or physical?
  - Single node, single region, or backbone-wide?

□ MINUTE 1-2: SCOPE THE BLAST RADIUS
  - How many customers/services are affected?
  - Is the impact growing or stable?
  - Check NOC dashboard / NMS for correlated alerts

□ MINUTE 2-3: CHECK RECENT CHANGES
  - Any maintenance windows active or recently closed?
  - Any config pushes in the last 4 hours?
  - Any software upgrades?
  - If yes → revert first, analyze later

□ MINUTE 3-4: INITIAL CONTAINMENT
  - If growing → isolate the affected domain (IGP overload, BGP drain)
  - If stable → begin protocol-specific diagnostics
  - If physical → dispatch fiber/hardware team, engage transport NOC

□ MINUTE 4-5: COMMUNICATE
  - Notify on-call / war room (Severity 1-2)
  - First status update: "We have [scope]. It is [growing/stable]. 
    We believe it is [control/data/physical]. Next update in [X] minutes."
  - Start the incident timeline NOW — not later, NOW
```

## 2. Incident Classification for Service Providers

Generic ITIL severity models don't work for SP networks. "Severity 1: Business-critical application unavailable" means nothing when your business IS the network and you have 400 customers with different SLAs on different services traversing different paths.

### SP-Specific Severity Model

| Severity | Criteria | Response | Escalation | SLA Clock |
|----------|----------|----------|------------|-----------|
| **SEV-1** | Backbone partition, multi-region impact, or >10% of customer base affected. Control plane instability spreading. | All-hands bridge. Dedicated incident commander. No other work until resolved. | VP/Director notified within 15 minutes. Customer comms within 30 minutes. | Starts immediately. Every minute counts for SLA credits. |
| **SEV-2** | Single region or POP offline. Major customer impacted. Redundancy exhausted (single points of failure exposed). | War room. 2-3 engineers engaged. Escalation to SMEs if no progress in 30 minutes. | Manager notified within 30 minutes. Affected customers notified within 1 hour. | Starts on detection. |
| **SEV-3** | Single link or node failure with successful failover. Customer impact minimal (sub-second convergence worked). Redundancy degraded. | On-call engineer. Diagnose and repair within maintenance window or next business day. | Ticket created. Manager notified next business day. | Repair SLA only (restore redundancy within N hours). |
| **SEV-4** | Cosmetic or non-impacting anomaly. Telemetry discrepancy. Degraded but within tolerance. | Ticket and investigate during normal hours. | None unless it persists or recurs. | None. |

### What Makes SP Classification Different

**1. "Impact" means different things at different layers.**

A single fiber cut might be:
- SEV-4 at the transport layer (DWDM protection switched, zero packet loss)
- SEV-3 at the IP layer (ECMP rebalanced, capacity reduced but sufficient)
- SEV-1 at the customer layer (one major customer's primary + backup both rode that fiber)

Always classify from the customer perspective, then work backward to the infrastructure perspective. The fiber cut is only SEV-4 if NO customer lost service AND no redundancy was fully consumed.

**2. "Growing" always outranks "current scope."**

A single adjacency flapping is SEV-3 — until you realize it's causing IS-IS SPF storms that are hitting CPU limits on three other nodes. The moment an incident shows signs of spreading, it escalates regardless of current blast radius.

**3. Customer diversity matters more than customer count.**

Losing one wholesale customer who buys a 100G wave and serves 50,000 downstream subscribers is worse than losing 200 retail broadband customers on a single access ring. The SLA exposure, the revenue impact, and the reputational damage are not proportional to the number of tickets.

### Severity Escalation Triggers

Incidents escalate when:

```
SEV-3 → SEV-2:
  - Second failure in the same fault domain (redundancy consumed)
  - Failure duration exceeds repair SLA
  - Customer escalation received
  - Automated failover shows signs of instability

SEV-2 → SEV-1:
  - Blast radius expanding beyond initial scope
  - Loss of management-plane access to affected nodes
  - Control-plane instability detected (route oscillation, adjacency cycling)
  - Duration exceeds 30 minutes with no root cause identified
  - Multiple simultaneous customer escalations
```

## 3. Protocol-Specific Diagnostic Flows

Generic troubleshooting ("check the interface, check the route, traceroute") doesn't scale in a backbone. Each protocol has its own failure modes, its own diagnostic artifacts, and its own recovery procedures. This section gives you the specific diagnostic flow for each.

### 3.1 BGP Incident Diagnostics

BGP incidents split into three categories: **session failures**, **route anomalies**, and **policy violations**. The diagnostic path is different for each.

#### Session Failures

```
BGP SESSION DOWN
        │
        ├─ Was it administratively shut?
        │    → show bgp neighbor <x> | include "Admin"
        │    → If yes: intentional. Check change log.
        │
        ├─ Is the TCP path healthy?
        │    → ping/traceroute to neighbor address
        │    → If unreachable: underlay problem (go to IGP diagnostics)
        │
        ├─ Is the hold timer expiring?
        │    → Check "Last message received" timestamp
        │    → If stale: peer is not sending keepalives
        │    → Possible: peer CPU overload, process crash, or unidirectional link
        │
        ├─ Did the peer send a NOTIFICATION?
        │    → IOS-XR: show bgp neighbor <x> | include "Error"
        │    → Junos: show bgp neighbor <x> | match "Last Error"
        │    → Decode the notification code:
        │        Cease/Admin Shutdown (6/2): Peer shut us down intentionally
        │        Cease/Max Prefix (6/1): We hit the prefix limit
        │        Update Message Error (3/x): Malformed update — parser bug or corruption
        │        Hold Timer Expired (4/0): Keepalives stopped arriving
        │        FSM Error (5/0): State machine desync — usually a software bug
        │
        └─ Is BFD involved?
             → BFD can tear down BGP before the hold timer fires
             → Check BFD session state: 
                IOS-XR: show bfd session | include <neighbor-ip>
                Junos: show bfd session | match <neighbor-ip>
             → If BFD is down but ICMP ping works: unidirectional fault,
                micro-loop, or BFD packet deprioritization under CPU load
```

#### Route Anomalies

Route anomalies are harder than session failures because the session is up but the routes are wrong.

```
EXPECTED ROUTE MISSING OR WRONG PATH
        │
        ├─ Is the route in the RIB at all?
        │    → IOS-XR: show route <prefix>
        │    → Junos: show route <prefix> detail
        │    → If missing entirely: check BGP for the prefix
        │
        ├─ Is the route in BGP but not installed?
        │    → show bgp <prefix> (check for "not installed" or "hidden")
        │    → Common causes:
        │        - More specific static/connected route wins
        │        - Next-hop unreachable
        │        - Route filtered by import policy
        │        - Route dampened (check dampening state)
        │
        ├─ Is the route present but via wrong path?
        │    → show bgp <prefix> detail → check AS-PATH
        │    → Expected path should have specific AS sequence
        │    → If path is different: upstream sent a different route
        │    → Check: did your peer change their export policy?
        │    → Check: is there a route leak from another peer/customer?
        │    → Check: are all your iBGP sessions up? Missing RR session
        │        can cause path selection to pick the "only path available"
        │
        └─ Is the route present, correct path, but traffic black-holed?
             → The route and forwarding table disagree
             → IOS-XR: show cef <prefix> (check adjacency, labels)
             → Junos: show route forwarding-table destination <prefix>
             → If FIB shows "drop" or "discard": programming failure
             → If FIB shows correct NH but packets still lost: 
                downstream issue (recurse through next-hops)
```

#### Route Leak Detection and Response

Route leaks are among the most damaging BGP incidents because they affect traffic flows across the entire internet, not just your backbone.

```
SUSPECTED ROUTE LEAK
        │
        ├─ Symptom: Unexpected routes from a peer/customer
        │    → show bgp neighbor <x> received-routes (if soft-reconfig)
        │    → Look for prefixes that shouldn't come from this neighbor
        │    → Check: are customer routes appearing via peer sessions?
        │    → Check: are peer routes appearing via customer sessions?
        │
        ├─ Confirm: Is it a leak or a legitimate path change?
        │    → Compare AS-PATH to expected patterns
        │    → A customer sending transit routes has an AS-PATH 
        │      that includes ASNs they shouldn't be providing transit for
        │    → RPKI validation:
        │      IOS-XR: show bgp rpki table <prefix>
        │        (syntax varies by release — some use show bgp origin-as validity)
        │      Junos: show validation database <prefix>
        │      (Invalid = almost certainly a leak or hijack)
        │
        ├─ Containment:
        │    → Apply max-prefix limits if not already configured
        │    → Apply AS-PATH filters to reject unexpected transit
        │    → Apply RPKI-based filtering (reject Invalid)
        │    → If severe: administratively shut the leaking session
        │    → Notify the peer/customer immediately
        │
        └─ Recovery:
             → Once the leak source is resolved, clear the session
             → Monitor for 15 minutes to confirm stability
             → Check that all prefixes are back on expected paths
             → Verify no persistent dampening of legitimate routes
```

### 3.2 IGP Storm Diagnostics

IGP storms — IS-IS or OSPF flooding events that hammer CPU and destabilize the entire routing domain — are the backbone equivalent of a distributed denial-of-service attack from inside your own network.

#### Recognizing a Storm

```
SYMPTOMS OF AN IGP STORM:
  - RPD/ISIS CPU at 80-100% on multiple nodes simultaneously
  - SPF computation running repeatedly (>5 SPF/minute sustained)
  - Adjacency flaps on nodes that should be stable
  - Log messages showing LSP/LSA flooding at abnormal rates
  - BGP sessions dropping due to keepalive timeout (CPU too busy for BGP)

# IOS-XR: Check SPF run count and frequency
show isis spf-log

# Junos: Check SPF computation history
show isis spf log

# What to look for:
# - SPF triggers coming from the SAME source repeatedly
# - LSP/LSA sequence numbers incrementing rapidly on one node
# - Short-lived LSPs (generated, flooded, then purged) repeating
```

#### Diagnostic Flow

```
IGP STORM DETECTED
        │
        ├─ Is one node generating most of the LSPs/LSAs?
        │    → IOS-XR: show isis database detail | include "Seq"
        │    → Junos: show isis database extensive | match "Sequence"
        │    → Look for one system-ID with rapidly incrementing seq numbers
        │    → If yes: that node is the storm source
        │
        ├─ Is the source node flapping an interface?
        │    → Interface bouncing causes LSP/LSA regeneration on each transition
        │    → show interface <x> | include "changes|flaps|resets"
        │    → Fix the interface (replace optic, disable/enable, investigate physical)
        │
        ├─ Is the source node in a purge-regeneration loop?
        │    → A node generates an LSP, another node purges it, source regenerates
        │    → This is usually a system-ID conflict (two nodes with same ID)
        │    → Or a partition where a node's LSP ages out, partition heals, 
        │      stale LSP gets purged, node regenerates, repeat
        │    → Check: show isis database | verify no duplicate system-IDs
        │
        ├─ Is it a DIS/DR election storm? (broadcast networks only)
        │    → DIS/DR flapping causes flooding on the entire broadcast segment
        │    → Verify priority settings are stable
        │    → Consider converting to point-to-point subinterfaces
        │
        └─ Containment (if source identified but fix takes time):
             → Set the source node to IS-IS overload
                IOS-XR: router isis <tag> → set-overload-bit
                Junos: set protocols isis overload
             → This drains traffic off the node without removing it from 
                the topology — it still floods LSPs but transit paths avoid it
             → Note: overload does NOT stop LSP generation. If the node is
                the storm source, overload buys time but doesn't stop flooding.
                For that, you may need to isolate the node (shut ISIS interfaces).
```

#### SPF Throttling — Your Emergency Brake

Both IOS-XR and Junos support SPF throttling to prevent one storm from consuming all CPU:

```
# IOS-XR: SPF interval throttling
router isis CORE
  address-family ipv4 unicast
    spf-interval 5000 50 200
  address-family ipv6 unicast
    spf-interval 5000 50 200

# Junos: SPF delay parameters
protocols isis {
    spf-options {
        delay 50;
        holddown 5000;
        rapid-runs 3;
    }
}

# Translation:
# Initial SPF runs immediately (50ms delay)
# Second SPF waits 200ms (IOS-XR) or runs immediately if within rapid-runs (Junos)
# Subsequent SPFs back off exponentially up to max 5000ms
# This prevents CPU from being consumed by continuous SPF recalculations
```

#### LSP/LSA Generation Throttling

Equally important — throttle how fast a node generates new LSPs/LSAs:

```
# IOS-XR: LSP generation interval (positional: max initial secondary)
router isis CORE
  lsp-gen-interval 5000 50 200

# Junos: LSP generation is throttled internally by the IS-IS implementation.
# lsp-lifetime and lsp-refresh-interval control LSP validity and refresh
# timing — they do NOT throttle burst generation from topology changes.
# Junos handles burst throttling internally via the RPD process.
protocols isis {
    lsp-lifetime 65535;
    lsp-refresh-interval 60000;  /* Refresh interval — not a storm prevention knob */
}
# Note: For actual storm mitigation on Junos, rely on spf-options (above)
# and the RPD's built-in LSP generation rate limiting.
```

### 3.3 MPLS/RSVP-TE Incident Diagnostics

RSVP-TE failures cascade. A single link failure can trigger preemption across dozens of LSPs, which redistribute bandwidth, which preempt more LSPs, which trigger more reoptimization, which causes oscillation. Understanding the cascade mechanics is more important than understanding any individual LSP failure.

#### Preemption Cascade Recognition

```
PREEMPTION CASCADE IN PROGRESS:
  - Multiple LSPs transitioning to Down or Suboptimal simultaneously
  - Rapid succession of ResvTear messages across multiple nodes
  - Auto-bandwidth adjustments triggering on many LSPs at once
  - Traffic shifting unpredictably between remaining paths
  - Bandwidth utilization spikes on surviving links

# IOS-XR: Check for mass LSP state changes
show mpls traffic-eng tunnels brief | include "Down|Reopt"

# Junos: Check for LSP flapping
show mpls lsp brief | match "Dn|Trans"

# Check the preemption log:
# IOS-XR
show mpls traffic-eng tunnels preemption-log

# Junos  
show mpls lsp extensive | match "Preempt"
```

#### The Cascade Mechanics

Understanding why preemption cascades happen:

```
Preemption basics:
  - Each RSVP-TE LSP has a SETUP priority and a HOLD priority (0-7, lower = higher)
  - A new LSP can only preempt an existing LSP if its SETUP priority is higher
    (lower number) than the existing LSP's HOLD priority
  - Example: LSP with setup=3 can preempt LSP with hold=5, but NOT hold=2
  - This is why "shut low-priority LSPs" works as a containment strategy

Cascade sequence:
1. Link A-B fails
2. LSPs traversing A-B reroute via alternative paths (C-D, E-F)
3. Alternative paths now carry original traffic + rerouted traffic
4. If total demand exceeds available bandwidth on C-D:
   - Higher priority LSPs preempt lower priority ones on C-D
   - Preempted LSPs try to find new paths
   - If their new paths also have bandwidth pressure → more preemption
5. This continues until either:
   - All LSPs find stable paths (convergence)
   - Some LSPs have no valid path (stuck in Down state)
   - The oscillation dampens naturally
   - Or it doesn't, and you have a sustained oscillation
```

#### Breaking the Cascade

```
CONTAINMENT STEPS:
1. Identify the trigger event
   → What link/node failed? When?
   → show log | include "LINK|DOWN|RSVP" (with timestamps)

2. Freeze non-critical LSPs
   → Administratively shut low-priority LSPs to free bandwidth
   → This creates headroom for high-priority LSPs to stabilize
   → IOS-XR: interface tunnel-te<N> → shutdown
   → Junos: deactivate protocols mpls label-switched-path <name>

3. Disable auto-bandwidth temporarily
   → Auto-bandwidth adjustments during a cascade make it worse
   → They keep changing reservations, triggering more CSPF recomputes
   → IOS-XR: mpls traffic-eng auto-bw timers frequency 86400
     (set to 24 hours to effectively suspend auto-bw during the incident)
   → Junos: set protocols mpls label-switched-path <name> auto-bandwidth 
            adjust-interval 86400  (effectively disabling)

4. Wait for stabilization (2-5 minutes)
   → Monitor LSP states until no new transitions occur

5. Gradually restore frozen LSPs
   → Bring them back one at a time, monitoring bandwidth impact
   → Re-enable auto-bandwidth only after all LSPs are stable
```

### 3.4 SRv6 Path Diagnostics

SRv6 incidents look different from MPLS incidents because there are no label tables to query, no RSVP sessions to inspect, and no per-LSP state machines. What you have instead is IPv6 forwarding with Segment Routing Headers, and the diagnostic challenge is that standard IPv6 troubleshooting tools often don't understand SRH.

#### SRv6 Forwarding Verification

```
# Step 1: Verify the SID is installed locally
# IOS-XR
show segment-routing srv6 sid

# Junos
show route table inet6.0 <SID-prefix>

# Step 2: Verify the locator is reachable via IGP
# IOS-XR
show isis route <locator-prefix>

# Junos
show isis route <locator-prefix>

# Step 3: Verify the SID function is programmed in FIB
# IOS-XR
show cef ipv6 <SID> detail

# Junos
show route forwarding-table destination <SID>

# Step 4: Trace the SRv6 path
# IOS-XR (SRv6 TE policy)
show segment-routing traffic-eng policy name <policy> detail

# Junos
show spring-traffic-engineering lsp detail
```

#### Common SRv6 Failure Modes

```
FAILURE                         SYMPTOM                          ROOT CAUSE

SID not in FIB                  Traffic dropped at headend       Locator not advertised in IGP,
                                                                 or SRv6 not enabled on the node

SRH not processed               Traffic forwarded as plain       Transit node doesn't support SRH,
                                IPv6 (wrong destination)         or SRv6 not enabled on interface

MTU exceeded                    Packets dropped silently         SRH adds 8+16*N bytes per segment;
                                (no ICMP if DF not set)          path MTU too small for encapsulated
                                                                 packet

Function mismatch               Traffic delivered to wrong       END vs END.DT6 vs END.DX6 —
                                VRF or interface                 wrong function allocated or
                                                                 advertised

Locator conflict                Intermittent forwarding          Two nodes advertising overlapping
                                or micro-loops                   locator blocks (same /48 assigned
                                                                 to different nodes)

FlexAlgo path missing           SRv6 policy down, no valid      FlexAlgo topology doesn't have a
                                SID for required algorithm       path — check algorithm definition
                                                                 and interface participation
```

#### SRv6-Specific Packet Capture

SRv6 packets look like IPv6 with a Routing Header Type 4. Most packet capture tools can decode them, but you need to know what you're looking at:

```
# Capturing SRv6 traffic:
# The outer IPv6 header has:
#   - Source: headend node address
#   - Destination: current active SID (changes at each segment endpoint)
#   - Next Header: 43 (Routing Header) if SRH present, or direct NH if last segment
#
# The SRH contains:
#   - Segments Left: how many SIDs remain to process
#   - Segment List[]: ordered list of SIDs (reversed — list[0] is the LAST SID)
#   - Current segment: list[Segments Left]

# IOS-XR: Capture with ACL-based packet capture
monitor capture POINT access-list SRV6-CAPTURE interface <intf> both

# Junos: Firewall filter + packet capture
set firewall family inet6 filter SRV6-CAPTURE term 1 from next-header routing-header
set firewall family inet6 filter SRV6-CAPTURE term 1 then count srv6-pkts
set firewall family inet6 filter SRV6-CAPTURE term 1 then accept

# Wireshark/tcpdump filter for SRH:
# ip6.nxt == 43 (Routing Header)
# Then check routing header type == 4 (SRH)
# tcpdump: ip6 protochain 43
```

### 3.5 Optical Layer Fault Diagnostics

Transport-layer faults are the most common trigger for IP-layer incidents, and they're also the ones most frequently misdiagnosed as IP problems. An engineer spends 30 minutes debugging an IS-IS adjacency loss when the real problem is a failing EDFA 200 km down the fiber path.

#### DOM-Based Triage

Digital Optical Monitoring is your first-line diagnostic for any interface that transitions to an error or down state:

```
# IOS-XR: Check optics levels
show controllers optics <intf>
    Key fields:
    - Rx Power (dBm): is it within the optic's receiver sensitivity range?
    - Tx Power (dBm): is the local transmitter within spec?
    - Temperature (C): is the optic overheating?
    - Voltage (V): is the optic getting clean power?
    - LBC (Laser Bias Current): rising LBC = aging laser = replace soon

# Junos: Check optics diagnostics
show interfaces diagnostics optics <intf>
    Key fields:
    - Laser output power
    - Laser receiver power  
    - Module temperature
    - Module voltage
    - Laser bias current
    - Rx/Tx power high/low alarm/warning thresholds

# Decision tree:
# Rx power within spec, Tx power within spec → Not an optical problem
# Rx power below threshold → Far-end transmitter issue or fiber path degradation
# Tx power below threshold → Local optic failing (replace it)
# Both Rx and Tx degraded → Check if the optic is overheating (temp alarm)
# Rx power shows sudden step-change → Fiber event (cut, macro-bend, dirty connector)
# Rx power slowly declining over weeks → Optic aging or fiber degradation (baseline drift)
```

#### FEC Error Analysis

Forward Error Correction corrects bit errors before they become frame errors. But FEC has a correction threshold — exceed it, and you get uncorrectable errors (frame drops).

```
# IOS-XR: FEC counters
show controllers optics <intf> | include "FEC|Corrected|Uncorrectable"

# Junos: FEC statistics
show interfaces <intf> extensive | match "FEC|Corrected|Uncorrectable"

# Interpretation:
#
# IMPORTANT: FEC thresholds depend on FEC TYPE. Modern coherent optics
# (100G/200G/400G) use Soft-Decision FEC (SD-FEC) with very different
# correction limits than legacy Hard-Decision FEC (HD-FEC, 10G/40G).
#
# HD-FEC (legacy 10G/40G, Reed-Solomon RS-255,239):
#   Pre-FEC BER < 1e-9:  Clean link, no concern
#   Pre-FEC BER 1e-9 to 1e-5: Link is degrading. Schedule maintenance.
#   Pre-FEC BER > 1e-5:  Approaching FEC correction limit (~8e-5). Imminent failure.
#
# SD-FEC (modern coherent 100G/200G/400G):
#   Pre-FEC BER < 1e-4:  Clean link, healthy margin
#   Pre-FEC BER 1e-4 to 1e-2: Link is degrading. Monitor Q-factor margin.
#   Pre-FEC BER > 2e-2:  Approaching SD-FEC correction limit (~2e-2 to 3.8e-2,
#                         varies by vendor/modulation). Imminent failure.
#
# For modern coherent systems, Q-factor margin (dB from the FEC threshold)
# is a more reliable metric than raw pre-FEC BER. Consult your optic vendor's
# data sheet (Acacia, Inphi/Marvell, Cisco NCS) for the specific SD-FEC
# correction limit of your deployed optics.
#
# Any uncorrectable FEC errors: Active data corruption. Act now.
#
# Note: "Corrected" FEC errors are not bad — that's FEC doing its job.
# Only uncorrectable errors indicate a real problem.
# But a rising trend in corrected errors predicts future uncorrectable errors.
```

## 4. Packet Capture at Scale

Packet capture on a 100G backbone interface is not the same as running tcpdump on a Linux box. The data rates, the encapsulation complexity, and the operational risk of capture are all different.

### The Fundamental Problem

At 100 Gbps, a full capture generates approximately 12.5 gigabytes per second. You can't capture everything, you can't store everything, and you can't analyze everything. Backbone packet capture is always selective.

### Capture Methods

```
METHOD              SPEED LIMIT    RISK              USE CASE

Port mirror/SPAN    Line rate      Traffic duplication   Full packet analysis,
                                   consumes bandwidth    but only if you have
                                                         a capture appliance
                                                         that handles 100G

ACL-based capture   Line rate      ACL processing cost,  Selective capture of
(on-box)            (sampled)      limited buffer,        specific flows or
                                   performance impact     prefixes

Inline TAP          Line rate      Physical insertion     Non-intrusive, but
                    (passive)      required (planned      requires physical
                                   maintenance)           access

Selective mirror    Line rate      Requires pre-planning, Capture specific
with filter         (filtered)     filter complexity      MPLS labels, SRv6
                                                          SIDs, or prefixes

sFlow/NetFlow       Sampled        Loses individual       Statistical analysis,
                    (1:N)          packet detail          not forensic capture
```

### MPLS Packet Capture Considerations

MPLS encapsulated packets are not visible to standard IP ACLs on most platforms. You need MPLS-aware capture:

```
# IOS-XR: Capture MPLS traffic on an interface
# Uses embedded packet capture (EPC)
monitor capture MPLS interface <intf> both
monitor capture MPLS match mpls
monitor capture MPLS start

# Junos: Capture with firewall filter
set firewall family mpls filter MPLS-CAPTURE term 1 from label <label-value>
set firewall family mpls filter MPLS-CAPTURE term 1 then count mpls-captured
set firewall family mpls filter MPLS-CAPTURE term 1 then sample

# Reading MPLS captures in Wireshark:
# - Outer Ethernet shows EtherType 0x8847 (MPLS Unicast)
# - Label stack: each label is 4 bytes (20-bit label, 3 TC, 1 S-bit, 8 TTL)
# - Bottom-of-stack bit (S=1) indicates last label
# - After the label stack: the inner packet (IP, IPv6, Ethernet for VPLS)
```

### SRv6 Capture Considerations

```
# SRv6 traffic is IPv6 — standard IPv6 capture works
# But you need to decode the SRH to understand what's happening

# Key fields in an SRv6 capture:
# 1. Outer IPv6 DA = currently active SID
# 2. SRH Segments Left = how far along the path we are
# 3. SRH Segment List = the full ordered path
# 4. Inner packet = the original customer traffic

# Wireshark display filter for SRv6:
# ipv6.routing.type == 4

# To see which SID is currently being processed:
# Active SID = Segment List[Segments Left]
# Next SID = Segment List[Segments Left - 1]
```

## 5. Failure Cascade Mechanics

Every experienced SP engineer has a story about a failure cascade — an incident where a simple event (one link down, one optic failure, one config push) turned into a multi-site, multi-hour outage through a chain of amplifying failures. Understanding cascade mechanics is what separates "we had a 5-minute failover" from "we had a 4-hour catastrophe."

### Anatomy of a Cascade

Cascades follow a predictable pattern even though each one feels unique:

```
TRIGGER EVENT
    │
    ├─ Primary failure: something breaks
    │
    ├─ Convergence: protocols react (good — this is what they're designed to do)
    │
    ├─ Load redistribution: traffic moves to surviving paths
    │
    ├─ AMPLIFICATION POINT: surviving paths can't handle the extra load
    │    │
    │    ├─ Queue overflow → packet drops on surviving links
    │    ├─ CPU overload → slow convergence on surviving nodes
    │    ├─ RSVP preemption → LSPs kicked off overloaded links
    │    ├─ BFD timeout → adjacency loss due to BFD packet deprioritization
    │    └─ Control plane contention → IGP/BGP keepalive misses
    │
    ├─ Secondary failures: amplified load causes new failures
    │
    └─ TERTIARY EFFECTS: secondary failures trigger their own convergence events
         → This is where a cascade becomes an outage
```

### Real-World Cascade Scenarios

#### Scenario 1: The ECMP Trap

```
Initial state:
  - 4x 100G links in an ECMP bundle between sites A and B
  - Average utilization: 60% per link (240G total traffic, 400G capacity)

Trigger: Link 1 fails
  - 3 links remain, 300G capacity
  - Traffic: 240G across 3 links = 80% utilization per link
  - Status: Fine. This is normal failover.

Second failure: Link 2 fails (unrelated optic failure, bad timing)
  - 2 links remain, 200G capacity  
  - Traffic: 240G across 2 links = 120% utilization
  - Queue drops begin on both surviving links
  - QoS kicks in — best-effort traffic dropped, priority traffic survives
  - But if priority traffic alone exceeds link capacity → everything drops

Key insight: The first failure was fine. The second failure was fatal.
This is why "N+1 redundancy" is not sufficient when N is small.
N+2 or load caps at 50% are the real protections against cascades.
```

#### Scenario 2: The BFD Amplifier

```
Trigger: CPU spike on Node X (software bug, memory leak, whatever)
  - BFD packets are processed in the data plane on most platforms
  - BUT: BFD packets from punt-path/slow-path interfaces use CPU
  - If CPU is overloaded → BFD packet processing is delayed
  - BFD detect multiplier * interval exceeded → BFD declares adjacency down
  - Adjacent node tears down IS-IS adjacency
  - IS-IS reconverges — SPF runs on ALL nodes in the area
  - SPF computation uses CPU on all nodes
  - If other nodes are also under CPU pressure → their BFD sessions risk timing out
  - CASCADE: CPU spike on one node → adjacency loss → SPF everywhere → 
    CPU spike everywhere → more BFD timeouts → more adjacency losses

Mitigation:
  - Use hardware-offloaded BFD (most modern platforms support this)
  - Set BFD timers appropriately (not too aggressive)
  - Use IS-IS overload on a node before it CPU-spirals, to drain traffic preemptively
  - Monitor CPU trends with telemetry — catch the spike before BFD does
```

#### Scenario 3: The Route Leak Bomb

```
Trigger: Customer BGP session leaks full table (800K+ routes) into your backbone
  - Your edge router accepts them (max-prefix not configured or too high)
  - Routes propagate via iBGP to all route reflectors
  - Route reflectors propagate to all PE routers
  - Every PE router processes 800K unexpected routes
  - FIB/TCAM fills up on routers with limited table capacity
  - FIB overflow → traffic to overflowed prefixes is dropped or punted to CPU
  - CPU-punted traffic adds to CPU load → potential BFD/keepalive impact
  
  Time from leak to backbone-wide impact: 30-90 seconds (iBGP convergence time)

Mitigation:
  - Max-prefix limits on EVERY customer session (no exceptions)
  - RPKI validation (reject Invalid routes)
  - AS-path filters (reject routes with unexpected transit ASNs)
  - IRR-based prefix filters (only accept registered prefixes)
  - BGP community-based scoping (mark customer routes, filter at RR)
```

### Cascade Prevention Checklist

```
INFRASTRUCTURE:
□ N+2 redundancy on critical backbone links (not just N+1)
□ Load caps: no link above 50% utilization during normal operations
□ Diverse fiber paths for parallel links (not just different lambdas)
□ UPS and generator diversity for multi-chassis POPs

PROTOCOL CONFIGURATION:
□ CoPP / control plane policing configured on all core nodes
  (Junos: lo0 firewall filter; IOS-XR: lpts pifib)
□ BFD with hardware offload where available
□ BFD timers not more aggressive than necessary (300ms x 3 is a good default)
□ IS-IS/OSPF SPF throttling configured (max 5-second holddown)
□ LSP/LSA generation throttling configured
□ BGP max-prefix limits on all external sessions
□ RSVP-TE preemption priorities reviewed (understand setup vs hold priority)
□ RSVP-TE FRR / TI-LFA for SR enabled on critical paths
□ Auto-bandwidth intervals not shorter than 5 minutes
□ IGP area/level design limits blast radius (avoid single flat L2 domain)
□ LDP-IGP synchronization enabled (prevents post-convergence black holes)

OPERATIONAL:
□ Monitoring for correlated failures (not just individual alarms)
□ CPU utilization trending with alerts at 60% sustained
□ Control-plane packet deprioritization awareness
□ Runbook for "cascade in progress — stop the bleeding"
□ Quarterly cascade scenario tabletop exercises
```

## 6. Post-Incident Forensics

The incident is over. The network is stable. Now you need to figure out what actually happened — not what you think happened during the fog of war, but what the data shows.

### Why Post-Incident Analysis Fails

Most RCAs fail for one of three reasons:

1. **The timeline is reconstructed from memory.** People remember what they did, not when they did it. Timestamps drift by minutes or hours. The sequence of events is guessed, not proven.

2. **The root cause is actually the most visible cause.** "The fiber was cut" is the visible cause. The root cause might be "our fiber diversity audit from 2019 wasn't updated when we added the new wavelength, so both paths rode the same conduit."

3. **The analysis stops at the first plausible explanation.** "BGP reconverged slowly" — why? "Because SPF was slow" — why? "Because CPU was high" — why was CPU high? Most RCAs stop two "whys" too early.

### Forensic Data Sources

```
                    memory, route counts         TSDB retention)
```

### Building an Honest Timeline

The timeline is the single most important artifact of post-incident analysis. Every subsequent conclusion depends on the sequence of events being correct.

#### Step 1: Anchor to Machine-Generated Timestamps

```
# Start with the FIRST machine-generated alert. 
# This is your T=0. Everything else is relative to this.

# Collect timestamps from ALL sources:
# - Syslog: grep for the incident window
# - Telemetry: query TSDB for anomalies in the incident window  
# - RSVP/BGP logs: filter by time range
# - Config archive: any commits during the window?
# - NMS: alarm history export

# CRITICAL: Verify all timestamps use the same time source (NTP/PTP).
# A 2-second clock skew between nodes can completely reorder 
# the sequence of events and lead to wrong conclusions.
```

#### Step 2: Construct the Event Chain

```
Build the chain as:

T+0s    [Node-A syslog]    Interface Gi0/0/0/1 down (link failure detected)
T+0.1s  [Node-A ISIS]      IS-IS adjacency down to Node-B
T+0.3s  [Node-B ISIS]      IS-IS adjacency down to Node-A (hold timer expired)
T+0.5s  [Node-A ISIS]      SPF computed — 12 routes changed
T+0.5s  [Node-B ISIS]      SPF computed — 12 routes changed  
T+0.7s  [Node-C ISIS]      LSP flood received from Node-A, SPF triggered
T+1.2s  [Node-D BGP]       Route <prefix> withdrawn by RR
T+1.5s  [Node-A RSVP]      3 LSPs rerouted via make-before-break
T+2.0s  [Node-E RSVP]      LSP preempted on link E-F (bandwidth exceeded)
...

# Each entry must have:
# - Timestamp (machine-generated, verified against NTP)
# - Source node
# - Protocol/subsystem
# - Event description
# - Causal link to previous event (where known)
```

#### Step 3: Identify the Five Whys

```
WHY #1: Why did customers lose service?
  → Because the backup path was also down.

WHY #2: Why was the backup path down?
  → Because both primary and backup rode the same fiber conduit.

WHY #3: Why were they on the same conduit?
  → Because the diversity audit wasn't updated when the backup was provisioned.

WHY #4: Why wasn't the audit updated?
  → Because there's no automated validation of path diversity.

WHY #5: Why isn't there automated validation?
  → Because we've never had a diversity failure before. (Until now.)

ROOT CAUSE: Lack of automated fiber diversity validation in the provisioning workflow.
TRIGGER: Fiber cut at [location].
CONTRIBUTING FACTOR: Manual diversity tracking with no verification step.
```

### The Post-Incident Report

Every post-incident report should contain these sections, in this order:

```
1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (3 sentences max)
   What happened, how long it lasted, who was affected.

2. TIMELINE
   Machine-anchored event chain (Step 2 above).

3. IMPACT
   - Services affected (which VPNs, which customers, which circuits)
   - Duration of impact (time to detection, time to mitigation, time to resolution)
   - SLA violations (if any)
   - Customer notifications sent

4. ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS
   - Five Whys chain
   - Root cause vs trigger vs contributing factors
   - Why existing safeguards didn't prevent this

5. WHAT WENT WELL
   - Detection time
   - Response coordination
   - Technical diagnosis
   - Communication

6. WHAT WENT WRONG
   - Gaps in monitoring
   - Slow escalation
   - Incorrect initial diagnosis
   - Communication failures

7. ACTION ITEMS
   - Each item has: description, owner, deadline, verification method
   - Categorized: prevent recurrence, improve detection, improve response
   - No action item should be "be more careful" — that's not actionable

8. APPENDIX
   - Raw logs
   - Configuration diffs
   - Packet captures
   - Topology diagrams showing the failure domain
```

## 7. Communication During Outages

Communication during a backbone outage is a separate skill from diagnosing the outage. The best engineer in the room is often the worst communicator during an incident — they're focused on the problem, not on the dozen people who need different information at different intervals.

### Three Audiences (Plus Three More)

The primary three audiences during any incident:

```
AUDIENCE         WHAT THEY NEED               WHAT THEY DON'T NEED

NOC / First      - Exact scope (what's down)   - Why it happened
Responders       - Is it growing?               - Protocol theory
                 - What to tell customers        - Speculation about root cause
                 - ETA for next update           - Technical jargon

Engineering      - Affected nodes/links         - Customer business impact
Team             - Protocol state (what's up,   - PR/communication concerns
                   what's down, what's flapping) - Management escalation details
                 - What's been tried             
                 - Current theory on root cause
                 - What data is needed next

Management       - Customer impact scope        - CLI output
                 - SLA exposure ($ at risk)      - Protocol details
                 - Is it getting better/worse?   - Debug methodology
                 - When will it be resolved?
                 - What do we tell the customer?
```

Three additional audiences that SP operations often forget:

```
AUDIENCE              WHAT THEY NEED               WHEN TO ENGAGE

Upstream/Peer SPs     - Route leak notifications     During any route leak,
                      - Major outage heads-up           hijack, or major outage
                      - Peering coordination            that affects shared traffic

Vendor TAC            - Case opened with             SEV-1: immediately
                        severity matching yours       SEV-2: within 30 minutes
                      - Bug identification            if root cause unclear
                      - Software workarounds

Regulatory / Public   - Mandatory notification        When required by SLA,
                        timelines (some SLAs and        regulation, or when the
                        jurisdictions require this)     outage is publicly visible
                      - Social media awareness
```

### The Status Update Template

During an active incident, status updates should follow a rigid format. Creativity is for postmortems, not bridge calls.

```
INCIDENT STATUS UPDATE — [Timestamp]
SEVERITY: [SEV-1/2/3]
INCIDENT ID: [ticket number]

CURRENT STATE: [One sentence — what is broken right now]

SCOPE: [Number of customers/services affected, regions involved]

TREND: [IMPROVING / STABLE / DEGRADING]

ACTIONS TAKEN:
  - [What was done since last update]
  - [Result of those actions]

NEXT STEPS:
  - [What will be tried next]
  - [Who is doing it]

ETA: [Next update in X minutes] or [Resolution ETA: unknown / approximately X]
```

### Communication Anti-Patterns

```
DON'T:
  - Say "we're working on it" without saying what you're working on
  - Give an optimistic ETA to make people feel better (it backfires when you miss it)
  - Share root cause theories before they're confirmed (they become "facts" immediately)
  - Let the bridge call go silent for more than 15 minutes
  - Use protocol acronyms with non-technical audiences
  - Blame a person or team during the incident (save it for the blameless postmortem)
  - Say "this has never happened before" (it's never helpful and usually not true)

DO:
  - Update at fixed intervals even if nothing has changed ("No change since last update. 
    Still investigating [specific thing]. Next update at [time].")
  - Assign one person as communications lead (separate from the person debugging)
  - Maintain a written timeline in real time (shared doc, incident channel)
  - Acknowledge customer impact explicitly ("We are aware that [specific services] 
    are affected for [specific customers]")
  - Give a range for ETAs ("We expect resolution between 30-60 minutes based on 
    current progress")
```

### The Incident Commander Role

For SEV-1 incidents, one person owns the incident. Not the diagnosis, not the fix — the incident. This person:

```
INCIDENT COMMANDER RESPONSIBILITIES:
  - Declares severity level and escalation
  - Assigns roles: who is debugging, who is communicating, who is documenting
  - Controls the bridge call (keeps it focused, prevents side conversations)
  - Makes containment decisions ("shut the session," "revert the change," "isolate the node")
  - Approves all customer-facing communications
  - Decides when the incident is resolved
  - Does NOT debug the problem themselves (their job is coordination)

WHO SHOULD BE IC:
  - Senior engineer or architect who understands the network
  - Someone who can make decisions under pressure
  - NOT the person who caused the incident (conflict of interest, real or perceived)
  - NOT the most junior person on call (they need to learn, not command)
```

## 8. War Stories: Cascade Patterns That Actually Happened

> These are composite scenarios based on common SP incidents. The details are generalized but the failure patterns are real.

### War Story 1: The Software Upgrade That Wasn't Hitless

```
Environment: Multi-vendor backbone, 20 core nodes, IS-IS + RSVP-TE
Event: Scheduled ISSU on Node-7 (IOS-XR, dual-RP)

T+0s     ISSU initiated on Node-7
T+45s    NSF handover to standby RP — IS-IS graceful restart triggered
         (Note: NSR would have replicated state without triggering GR.
          This scenario uses NSF, which relies on GR helper cooperation.)
T+46s    Adjacent nodes enter IS-IS helper mode (expected)
T+90s    Graceful restart timer expires on Node-12 
         (Node-12 running a different vendor's implementation)
         Node-12 tears down the adjacency to Node-7 (unexpected)
T+91s    IS-IS reconverges around Node-7 (treats it as failed)
T+92s    RSVP-TE LSPs through Node-7 reroute
T+93s    But Node-7 is actually UP — it's still forwarding traffic
         Traffic arrives at Node-7 via RSVP-TE (not rerouted yet on ingress)
         Node-7 forwards it toward Node-12
         Node-12 has no IS-IS adjacency to Node-7 — drops the traffic
T+94s    Black hole forms: ingress still sends via Node-7, Node-12 drops
T+120s   RSVP-TE LSPs on ingress finally reroute around Node-7
T+121s   Black hole clears
T+180s   Node-7 ISSU completes, IS-IS adjacency re-established
T+200s   Traffic returns to normal paths

Impact: 30 seconds of packet loss during a "hitless" upgrade
Root cause: Graceful restart timer mismatch between vendors
Lesson: Test ISSU in a multi-vendor environment. Verify GR timer
        consistency across ALL adjacent nodes BEFORE the maintenance window.
```

### War Story 2: The Monitoring System That Caused the Outage

```
Environment: 100-node backbone, gNMI streaming telemetry to collector cluster
Event: Telemetry collector ran out of disk space at 3am

T+0s     Collector stops accepting gNMI streams (TCP backpressure)
T+5s     gNMI sessions from routers to collector start buffering
T+30s    Router-side gNMI buffer fills up on 40 nodes
T+31s    Different vendors handle this differently:
           - Vendor A: drops telemetry, no impact on routing (correct behavior)
           - Vendor B: TCP session blocks, process hangs, shared thread pool 
             includes NETCONF — NETCONF server becomes unresponsive
T+60s    Automated change system pushes scheduled config via NETCONF to 12 nodes
         (all Vendor B)
T+61s    NETCONF timeout on all 12 nodes — change system logs errors
T+62s    Change system retry logic kicks in — retries all 12 simultaneously
T+90s    On 3 nodes, the original config push partially applied before the hang
         (Note: platforms with atomic commit semantics — IOS-XR candidate config,
          Junos candidate config — should prevent partial application. This scenario
          reflects older or non-atomic config models. Atomic commit is a key safety
          mechanism that should be verified per-platform.)
         These 3 nodes have incomplete configurations
T+91s    One of the partial configs removed a route-policy from a BGP session
         → Customer prefixes accepted without filtering
         → 12,000 unexpected routes flood into the backbone
T+95s    Route churn propagates to all RRs and all PE nodes
T+120s   FIB updates across the backbone — brief traffic disruption
T+180s   NOC gets paged (3 minutes late because the paging system also uses 
         the collector cluster for threshold evaluation)

Impact: 3 minutes of packet loss, 15 minutes of degraded service
Root cause: Monitoring system failure cascaded into config management failure
Lesson: Monitoring infrastructure must be treated as production infrastructure.
        Telemetry buffer behavior must be validated per-vendor.
        Config automation must have circuit breakers (fail-safe on error, not retry).
```

### War Story 3: The Label Table That Ran Out of Room

```
Environment: MPLS backbone, 200K LSPs, LDP + RSVP-TE coexistence
Event: New customer deployment added 15,000 VPN routes

T+0s     15,000 new VPN routes pushed via BGP
T+10s    PE routers allocate VPN labels — 15,000 new labels per PE
T+15s    Core router (transit, not PE) receives 15,000 new LDP labels for the
         new PE loopbacks... wait, that's only 3 new labels (one per new PE)
T+16s    PE-3 label table at 98% of MPLS forwarding table capacity
         (PE-3 is an older chassis with a smaller TCAM)
T+17s    Next 500 label allocations exceed TCAM limit on PE-3
T+18s    PE-3 starts logging: "MPLS forwarding table full"
         New labels not installed in hardware FIB
         Software forwarding attempted → CPU at 100%
T+20s    BFD sessions on PE-3 time out (CPU can't process BFD packets)
T+21s    IS-IS adjacencies drop on PE-3
T+22s    All customer VPNs on PE-3 lose connectivity
T+25s    RSVP-TE LSPs transit through PE-3 reroute
T+30s    Traffic shifts to other PE routers, which were already at 85% capacity

Impact: PE-3 completely offline, remaining PEs overloaded
Root cause: No TCAM capacity monitoring, no pre-deployment capacity check
Lesson: Monitor FIB/TCAM/label utilization as a first-class metric.
        Pre-validate capacity impact of new deployments.
        PE chassis refresh cycles should be driven by table capacity, 
        not just throughput or age.
```

```
rate(interface_bytes_total{direction="egress"}[5m]) * 8 > 80e9

# Alert: any LSP changed state in last 5 minutes

mpls_fwd_table_used / mpls_fwd_table_total > 0.85
```

## 10. Tabletop Exercises

You wouldn't deploy a new protocol without testing it. Why would you deploy an incident response plan without testing it?

### Exercise Format

```
TABLETOP EXERCISE STRUCTURE:

1. Scenario briefing (5 minutes)
   - "At 2:17am, the NOC receives alarms for..."
   - Participants don't know the root cause

2. Round 1: Initial triage (10 minutes)
   - What do you check first?
   - What severity do you assign?
   - Who do you call?

3. Inject 1: New information (the scenario evolves)
   - "Five minutes later, you also see..."
   - Does the severity change?
   - What's your updated theory?

4. Round 2: Diagnosis and containment (15 minutes)
   - Walk through the diagnostic flow
   - What commands would you run? On which nodes?
   - What's your containment strategy?

5. Inject 2: Complication
   - "The change you made didn't work, and now..."
   - How do you adapt?

6. Debrief (15 minutes)
   - What went well?
   - Where did the team hesitate?
   - What knowledge gaps were exposed?
```
